%% Abstract
\begin{abstract}
  This study presents a constrained optimization-based neuro-adaptive control (CONAC) for a class of unknown multi-input-multi-output (MIMO) systems subject to multiple convex input constraints. 
  A deep neural network (DNN) is employed to approximate the ideal control law while handling multiple convex input constraints within a unified constrained optimization framework.
  The adaptive variables, including the DNN weights and Lagrange multipliers\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, }\DIFaddend are updated through adaptation law\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{s}\DIFdelend  derived from the formulated constrained optimization problem, yielding Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions at equilibrium.
  The controller's stability is rigorously analyzed using Lyapunov theory, establishing the uniform ultimate boundedness (UUB) of the tracking errors and adaptive variables.
  The proposed controller is compared with existing methods through real-time experiments on a 2-degree-of-freedom (DOF) robotic manipulator, highlighting its superior capability to handle input \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{saturation }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{constraints }\DIFaddend and its feasibility in real-time implementation.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
  Neuro-adaptive control, constrained optimization, deep neural network,  multiple convex input constraints.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}

\IEEEPARstart{M}{any} control systems are constrained by various safety requirements, actuator limitations, and physical restrictions \cite{Ji:2025aa}.
These constraints often manifest as convex input constraints, which can be in the form of nonlinear constraints rather than the element-wise (symmetric or asymmetric) constraints \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, }%DIFDELCMD < \ie 
%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdel{simple box constraints }\DIFdelend that limit each input independently, \ie $\underline{u}_i\le u_i\le\overline{u}_i$, $\forall i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}$, where $\underline{u}_i,\overline{u}_i\in\R$ denote the lower and upper bounds of the $i$th input $u_i$, respectively.
\DIFdelbegin %DIFDELCMD < \MSRY{여기서 element-wise를 먼저 언급하였습니다; 마찬가지로 문헌에서 많이 쓰이는 symmetric/asymmetric도 언급하였습니다.}
%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdelend For example, under road friction limits, the admissible global force and moment of vehicles can be characterized by a weighted norm bound, which yields a convex ellipsoidal constraint \cite{Chen:2024aa}.
In motor drive systems, the feasible voltage region is often represented by circular \cite{Ryu:2025aa} or hexagonal sets \cite{Monzen:2026aa}.
Furthermore, admissible input sets may be determined by multiple constraints that must hold simultaneously, such as element-wise constraints combined with norm constraints, which can be represented as the intersection of convex sets.
Therefore, addressing these multiple convex input constraints is crucial for ensuring the safety and reliability of control systems in real-world applications.

In parallel, control systems often involve uncertain or even unknown dynamics due to modeling errors, unmodeled dynamics, and external disturbances.
Although adaptive control provides a systematic mechanism for online adaptation of controllers \cite{Ioannou:2006aa}, conventional formulations often rely on structural assumptions about the uncertain or unknown dynamics, which may not be available in practice.
Neuro-adaptive control (NAC) alleviates this assumption by using neural networks (NNs) to approximate the dynamics in a structured and parameterized representation \cite{Farrell:2006aa,Patil:2022aa}.
Like adaptive control, NAC methods ensure closed-loop stability using Lyapunov theory, and the NN weights are adapted online without prior knowledge of the system.
The NAC methods have shown promising results in various real-world applications, including vehicle control \cite{You:2023aa} and flexible robotic manipulators \cite{Gao:2026aa}.

Because multiple convex input constraints and uncertain or unknown dynamics often coexist in real-world applications, incorporating multiple input constraint handling into NAC is essential.
This motivates a review of existing input constraint handling methods in NAC and related constrained control frameworks.

\subsubsection{Structural Methods for Input Constraint Handling in NAC}

% Since the NAC framework is based on Lyapunov theory, structural methods have been widely studied for input constraint handling.
Since the NAC framework is based on Lyapunov theory, input constraints have been primarily handled using structural methods in the NAC literature.
\DIFdelbegin %DIFDELCMD < \MSRY{NAC는 Lyapunov에 기반 $\to$ 입력제약을 구조적 방법으로 처리하면 Lyapunov로 해석 가능 $\to$ NAC가 구조적 방법으로 입력제약을 처리; 라는 흐름을 하고 싶었습니다; 단순히 NAC $\to$ 구조적 방법 (X) NAC $\to$ 입력제약을 구조적방법으로 처리 (O)}
%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdelend In \cite{Ji:2025aa}, Ji \etal categorized the literature on the structural methods into two strategies: anti-saturation design and direct design.
We review these strategies in the context of NAC.

In anti-saturation design, a virtual auxiliary system is mainly constructed to compensate for the effects of input saturation \cite{Ma:2025aa,Gao:2026aa,Yang:2021aa,Jia:2020aa,Liu:2024aa,Liu:2025aa,Gao:2017aa,Esfandiari:2014aa,Esfandiari:2015aa,Yu:2022aa,Rezaei:2025aa,He:2016aa,He:2019aa,Zhao:2016aa,Peng:2020aa}.
Owing to their high compatibility, auxiliary systems have been adopted in various control designs.
For example, auxiliary systems have been incorporated into backstepping-based NAC \cite{Gao:2017aa} and sliding mode control (SMC)-based NAC \cite{Ma:2025aa} to mitigate the effects of input saturation.
Furthermore, the auxiliary systems have been integrated into reinforcement learning (RL)-based NAC designs \cite{Gao:2026aa} and barrier Lyapunov function (BLF)-based NAC designs \cite{Yang:2021aa}.
To achieve finite-time or fixed-time convergence, novel finite-time \cite{Jia:2020aa} or fixed-time \cite{Liu:2024aa,Liu:2025aa} auxiliary systems have been proposed.

Generally, when the control input reaches the saturation limit, the auxiliary system is excited, and the auxiliary state is fed back into the control law as additional compensation terms.
However, from a learning perspective, if the auxiliary state is not incorporated into the feedback error used in the adaptation law, the NN weights may generate excessive control input to achieve the desired tracking performance, since the saturation effect is not considered in the adaptation process.
Therefore, the auxiliary state is often considered in the adaptation law \cite{Gao:2017aa,Esfandiari:2014aa,Esfandiari:2015aa,Liu:2024aa,Liu:2025aa,Yu:2022aa,Rezaei:2025aa}.
This ensures that the NN weights adapt not only for approximating the ideal control law but also for compensating for the effect of input saturation.

On the other hand, in direct design, the saturation effects are directly incorporated into the controller design and stability analysis.
Typically, the saturation function is approximated by a smooth function, such as $\tanh(\cdot)$, and the mean value theorem is used to transform it into affine form \cite{Wang:2013aa,Cui:2014aa,Li:2024aa}.
However, as reported in \cite{Min:2021aa}, the smooth approximation of the saturation function may introduce conservative behavior and performance degradation, especially when the control input is close to the saturation limit, compared to the auxiliary system-based anti-saturation design.

\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Another approach can be found in reinforcement learning (RL)-based NAC \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{Zhang:2024ab,Qin:2025ab}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
.
The input saturation is handled by introducing a nonquadratic input cost into the infinite-horizon optimal control problem, which is solved using adaptive dynamic programming (ADP).
Within the resulting ADP framework, the optimal cost function is approximated by a critic neural network (CNN), and the optimal control law is derived from the Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs (HJI) equation or the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation.
Owing to the nonquadratic input cost, the resulting optimal control law is based on $\tanh(\cdot)$ and satisfies asymmetric or symmetric input bounds.
}

\DIFaddend Despite their effectiveness, the aforementioned structural methods are limited to element-wise input constraints, such as symmetric or asymmetric bounds on each input.
Thus, extending them to admissible input sets described by multiple convex constraints is not straightforward.

\subsubsection{Optimization-Based Methods for Input Constraint Handling}

Besides structural methods, optimization-based methods have been studied for constraint handling across a broad range of control design frameworks.
These methods compute the control input by solving constrained optimization problems at each time \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{instant}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{step}\DIFaddend , as in model predictive control (MPC) \cite{Vahidi-Moghaddam:2025aa,Mallick:2025aa,Khodaverdian:2025aa,Greune:2011aa,Bemporad:1999aa,Berberich:2025aa,Draeger:1995aa} and control barrier function (CBF)-based quadratic programming (QP) filters \cite{Ames:2019aa,Ko:2025aa,Sweatland:2025aa}. 
In these methods, the constrained control problem is explicitly formulated as a constrained optimization problem.
Therefore, complex nonlinear (even non-convex) input constraints can be systematically incorporated into the control design, which is a significant advantage over the structural methods.

Even though they solve constrained optimization problems at each time \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{instant}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{step}\DIFaddend , the computational burden may not necessarily be significant for small-scale QPs or MPC problems with short horizons and simple constraints.
However, it can increase substantially when multiple constraints must be handled simultaneously, nonlinear constraints are introduced, or the resulting problem is non-convex.
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{In such cases, real-time implementation may require iterative numerical solvers, suitable initialization, and careful control of the problem dimension and solver convergence.
In }\DIFdelend %DIF >  In such cases, real-time implementation may require iterative numerical solvers, suitable initialization, and careful control of the problem dimension and solver convergence.
\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{In }\DIFaddend this regard, some studies \cite{Vahidi-Moghaddam:2025aa,Mallick:2025aa,Khodaverdian:2025aa} trained the optimal constrained control policy offline using deep NNs (DNNs) to reduce online computation.

Moreover, closed-loop stability guarantees generally rely on additional analytical conditions specific to the selected optimization framework.
For MPC, these conditions commonly include recursive feasibility, appropriate terminal ingredients, sufficiently long prediction horizons, and computational tractability \cite{Greune:2011aa}.
In addition to these conditions, model accuracy is critical, thereby motivating robust \cite{Bemporad:1999aa}, data-driven \cite{Berberich:2025aa}, and learning-based MPC \cite{Draeger:1995aa,Vahidi-Moghaddam:2025aa} approaches.
Model accuracy is also crucial for CBF-QP filters, where the validity of the safety guarantee depends on the accuracy of the system dynamics used to formulate the barrier constraint.
Accordingly, neuro-adaptive system identifiers have been introduced to construct the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{CBF constraint }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{CBF-QP filters }\DIFaddend using approximated system dynamics \cite{Sweatland:2025aa}.

Although these approaches can provide stability or safety guarantees under their stated assumptions, the guarantees typically depend on feasibility, prediction, or \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{filtering mechanisms }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{safety-critical filters }\DIFaddend whose validity is affected by model accuracy, uncertainty descriptions, data quality, and offline training coverage.

\subsection{Motivation and Proposed Approach}

Both structural and optimization-based methods are effective for input constraint handling, but they have complementary limitations, as summarized in Table~\ref{table:intro:comparison}.
Structural methods offer Lyapunov-based stability guarantees and solver-free computation, but they are limited in terms of the classes of constraints they can accommodate.
Optimization-based methods can handle general constraints, but their closed-loop stability guarantees are often tied to additional conditions and iterative solvers may be required.

These observations motivate a unified NAC framework in which more complex input constraints are incorporated directly into the online adaptation law, as presented in \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{our }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the }\DIFaddend previous work \cite{Ryu:2025aa}.
In the proposed CONAC framework, the NAC problem is formulated as a constrained optimization problem in which multiple convex input constraints are imposed as inequality constraints.
The constrained optimization formulation is not used to solve a control allocation problem at each time \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{instant}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{step}\DIFaddend ; instead, it is used to derive continuous-time adaptation law\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{s}\DIFdelend  for the adaptive variables.
Therefore, the proposed framework \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{retains the Lyapunov-friendly structure of NAC while incorporating the constraint-handling }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{combines the Lyapunov-based stability analysis of structural methods with the broader input-constraint handling }\DIFaddend capability of optimization-based methods\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, particularly for multiple convex input constraints}\DIFaddend .

\subsection{Contributions}

The main contributions of this study are:
\begin{itemize}
  % \item 
  % A constrained optimization-based neuro-adaptive control (CONAC) framework is proposed for unknown systems under multiple convex input constraints;
  % The admissible input set is represented by convex inequality constraints and embedded directly into the online adaptation law;

  \item
  Any combination of convex input constraints can be handled, thereby significantly extending the applicability of the NAC to real-world systems with complex input constraints;

  \item 
  The adaptation law\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{s}\DIFdelend  for adaptive variables, including the DNN weights and the Lagrange multipliers\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{are }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, is }\DIFaddend systematically derived from the formulated constrained optimization problem, and the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions are satisfied at an equilibrium of the adaptation dynamics \cite[Chap. 12, Thm. 12.1]{Nocedal:2006aa}; and

  \item 
  The closed-loop stability under the proposed Constrained Optimization-Based Neuro-Adaptive Control (CONAC) is rigorously analyzed using Lyapunov theory, ensuring the uniform ultimate boundedness (UUB) of the tracking error and adaptive variables.

\end{itemize}

This study extends \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{our }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the }\DIFaddend preliminary work in \cite{Ryu:2025ab,Ryu:2025aa}, which introduced inequality constraints on the weight and input norms within a constrained optimization framework for NAC using single-hidden-layer NNs. 
In the present work, the same framework is generalized to accommodate multiple convex input constraints and extended to DNNs, while still ensuring Lyapunov-based stability guarantees.
Furthermore, unlike the previous study, which was limited to numerical simulations, the proposed CONAC is experimentally validated through real-time implementation on a 2-degree-of-freedom (DOF) robotic manipulator.

\section{Problem Formulation} \label{sec:problem:formulation}

We consider an unknown multi-input multi-output (MIMO) system modeled as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:sys}
  \ddtt\mv{x} 
  = 
  \mv{f}(\mv{x}) 
  + 
  \mm{B}(\mv{x})\mysat(\mv{u})
  ,
\end{equation}
where $\mv{x},\mv{u}\in\R^n$ denote the state and control input vectors, respectively.
The unknown system function and control effectiveness matrix are denoted by $\mv{f}:\R^n\to\R^n$ and $\mm{B}:\R^n\to\R^{n\times n}$, respectively, which are assumed to be locally Lipschitz continuous.
The saturation function is denoted by $\mysat:\R^n\to\Omega_u$, where $\Omega_u\subset\R^n$ is the convex admissible input set.

The system \eqref{eq:sys} is subject to the following assumptions.

\begin{assum} \label{assum:B:bound}
  The control effectiveness matrix $\mm{B}(\mv{x})$ is nonsingular and bounded as   
  $
    \underline{b}\mm{I}_n
    \preceq 
    \mm{B}(\mv{x})
    \preceq 
    \bar{b}\mm{I}_n
    ,
  $
  where $\underline{b},\bar{b}\in\R_{>0}$ are unknown positive constants.
\end{assum}

\DIFdelbegin %DIFDELCMD < \begin{assum}[{\cite[Assumption 1(b)]{Boulkroune:2010aa}}] %%%
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{assum}[{\cite[Assump. 1(b)]{Boulkroune:2010aa}}] \DIFaddend \label{assum:B:bound:grad}
  There exists an unknown positive function \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\beta(\mv{x}):\R^n\to\R_{>0}$ such that $\tfrac{1}{2}\mv{v}^\top\left(\ddtt\mm{B}(\mv{x})^{-1}\right)\mv{v} \le \beta(\mv{x})\norm{\mv{v}}^2$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\rho_{\beta}(\mv{x}):\R^n\to\R_{>0}$ such that $\tfrac{1}{2}\mv{v}^\top\left(\ddtt\mm{B}(\mv{x})^{-1}\right)\mv{v} \le \rho_{\beta}(\mv{x})\norm{\mv{v}}^2$}\DIFaddend , $\forall \mv{v}\in\R^n$, and $\forall \mv{x}\in\R^n$. 
\end{assum}

\begin{assum} \label{assum:sat}
  The \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{convex }\DIFaddend admissible input set \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\Omega_u$ }\DIFaddend and saturation function satisfy the following conditions:
  \begin{enumerate}

      \bigcap_{j\in\mathcal I}
      \left\{
        \mv u\in\R^n:
        c_j^u(\mv u)\le 0
      \right\}
      ,
    \end{equation*}
    where $\mathcal I$ denotes the input constraint index set.

    \item
    The origin lies in the interior of $\Omega_u$, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{i.e.,
    }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \ie
    \DIFaddend \begin{equation*}
      c_j^u(\mv 0)

        \norm{\mysat(\mv u)}

    \end{equation*}
  \end{enumerate}
\end{assum}

We review the DNN structure and its approximation capability, which are essential for the subsequent controller design and stability analysis.
According to the universal approximation property \cite{Patil:2022aa}, the DNN\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{s}\DIFdelend  $\NN: \mathbb{R}^{l_0+1} \times \mathbb{R}^{\Xi} \to \mathbb{R}^{l_{k+1}}$ can be used to approximate any smooth unknown nonlinear function $\mv{g}:\R^{l_0+1}\to\R^{l_{k+1}}$.
Specifically, there exists a DNN such that 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dnn:approx:error}
  \sup_{\NNin\in\Omega_{\NNin}}
  \norm{
    \mv{g}(\NNin) - \NN(\NNin;\wth^*)
  }
  =
  \sup_{\NNin\in\Omega_{\NNin}}
  \norm{
    \mv{\epsilon}
  }
  =
  \overline{\epsilon}
  ,
\end{equation}
where $\NNin \in \mathbb{R}^{l_0+1}$ is the DNN input vector and $\idealwth \in \mathbb{R}^{\Xi}$ denotes the ideal DNN weights that minimize the approximation error $\mv{\epsilon}\in\R^{l_{k+1}}$.
The DNN input vector $\NNin$ should be designed to include all arguments of the unknown function $\mv{g}$.
A compact set $\Omega_{\NNin} \subset \mathbb{R}^{l_0+1}$ denotes the approximation region of the DNN, which ensures the boundedness of $\idealwth$ and $\mv{\epsilon}$, such that $\norm{\idealwth} \le \overline{\theta}^*\in\R_{>0}$ and $\norm{\mv{\epsilon}} \le \overline{\epsilon} \in\R_{>0}$.
The DNN $\NN(\NNin;\wth)=\NN_k$ can be recursively defined as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dnn:def}
  \NN_i :=
  \begin{cases}
    \wV_i^\top \act_i(\NN_{i-1}), 
    &
    i\in\{1,\dots ,k\},
    \\
    \wV_0^\top \NNin,
    &
    i=0
    ,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\wV_i\in\R^{(l_i+1)\times l_{i+1}}$ and $\act_i: \R^{l_i}\to\R^{l_i+1}$ represent the weight matrix and the activation function of the $i$th layer for all $i\in\mathcal{K}$, respectively, and $\mathcal{K}:=\{0,1,\cdots,k\}$ denotes the index set of layers.
The DNN $\NN(\NNin;\wth)$ has $k+1$ layers, where the input and output layers are indexed by $i=0$ and $i=k$, respectively, as illustrated by the example in Fig.~\ref{fig:DNN}.
The activation function is defined as $\act_i(\mv{z}) = \left(\tanh(z_1),\cdots,\tanh(z_{l_i}),1\right)^\top,$ for some $\mv{z}\in\R^{l_i}$.
We simplify the notations by vectorizing all weight matrices as 
\begin{equation*}
  \wth_i 
  :=
  \myvec(\wV_i)
  \in\R^{(l_i+1)l_{i+1}}
  ,
  \qquad
  \forall 
  i\in\mathcal{K}
  ,
\end{equation*}
and, finally, stacking them as $\wth := \left[\wth_i\right]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}\in\R^\Xi$, where $\Xi := \sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}} (l_i+1)l_{i+1}$.
Additionally, like $\overline{\theta}^*$, there exists a maximum norm bound for the weights of each layer $\overline{\theta}^*_i\in\R_{>0}$, such that $\norm{\idealwth_i}\le\overline{\theta}^*_i$, $\forall i\in\mathcal{K}$.

The gradient of $ \NN(\NNin;\wth)$ with respect to $\wth$ can be obtained using Proposition~\ref{propsit:kron} and the chain rule as follows:
\begin{equation}
  \ppfrac{\NN}{\wth}=
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \ppdfrac{\NN}{\wth_k}&
    \ppdfrac{\NN}{\wth_{k-1}}&
\cdots &
    \ppdfrac{\NN}{\wth_0}
  \end{bmatrix}
  \in\R^{l_{k+1} \times \Xi}
  ,
  \label{eq:gradient:phi}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \ppfrac{\NN}{\wth_i} = 
  \begin{cases}
    % (
      \mm I_{l_{k+1}}
      \otimes 
      \act_{k}^\top  
    % )
    , 
    &
    i=k
    ,
    \\
    \wV_k^\top\act_{k}' 
    (
      \mm I_{l_{k}}
      \otimes  
      \act_{k-1}^\top  
    )
    , 
    & 
    i=k-1
    ,
    \\
    \vdots 
    &
    \vdots 
    \\
    \wV_k^\top\act'_{k} 
    \cdots 
    \wV_1^\top\act_1' 
    (
      \mm I_{l_1}
      \otimes 
      \NNin^\top  
    )
    , 
    &
    i = 0
    ,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:NN:grad}
\end{equation}
and $\act_i:= \act_i(\NN_{i-1})$ and $\act_i':= \ddtfrac{\act_i}{\NN_{i-1}}$, for all $i\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{0\}$.

\subsection{Adaptation Law Derivation} \label{sec:main:result:sub:adap:laws}

% The control objective which minimizes the tracking error $\mv{e}$ while satisfying both weight boundedness and input constraints can be represented as follows:
The control objective\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{s}\DIFdelend  defined in Section~\ref{sec:problem:formulation} can be formulated as a constrained optimization problem, as follows:
\begin{subequations} \label{eq:opt:prob} 
  \begin{align} 
    \min_{\estwth} \quad 
    & 
    J(\mv{e};\estwth)
    = 
    \tfrac{1}{2} \mv{e}^\top \mv{e} 
    &
    \label{eq:opt:prob:obj} 
    \\ 
    \text{subject to} \quad  
    & 
    c^{\theta}_i(\estwth_i)=\tfrac{1}{2}
    \left(
      \norm{\estwth_i}^2
      -
      \overline{\theta}_i^2 
    \right)
    \le 0, 
    \!
    &
    \!
    \forall i\in\mathcal{K} 
    ,
    \label{eq:opt:prob:const1} 
    \\ 
    & 
    c^{u}_j(\estwth)
    % c_j^u
    % \left(
    %     \mm{P}\NN(\NNin;\estwth)
    % \right) 
    \le 0, 
    &
    \forall j\in\mathcal{I} 
    \label{eq:opt:prob:const2} 
    ,
  \end{align}%
\end{subequations}
where $J(\mv{e};\estwth)$ is the objective function.
In the optimization problem, inequality constraints of weight boundedness $c^{\theta}_i(\estwth_i),\ \forall i\in\mathcal{K},$ and input constraints $c^{u}_j(\estwth),\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I},$ are imposed.
\DIFdelbegin %DIFDELCMD < \MSRY{inequality constraints of input constraints인데 괜찮을까요? 제가 의도한건 inequality constraints of weight boundedness and input saturation이긴했습니다.}
%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdelend 

Specifically, the input constraints used in \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const2} are the same convex constraint functions that characterize the admissible input set in Assumption~\ref{assum:sat}.
With a slight abuse of notation, the composed input constraints are denoted by
$c_j^u(\estwth):=c_j^u(\mv u(\estwth))=c_j^u(\mm P\NN(\NNin;\estwth)),\ \forall j\in\mathcal I$, to explicitly represent the dependence of the input constraints on the DNN weights $\estwth$.
Additionally, all active constraints are assumed to satisfy the Linear Independence Constraint Qualification (LICQ) to avoid degeneracy \cite[pp. 465--467]{Nocedal:2006aa}.

The Lagrangian function $L:=L(\mv{e},\estwth,[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}},[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal I})$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
  L
  = 
  J(\mv{e};\estwth) 
  + 
  \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
  \sigma_{i}
  c^{\theta}_{i}(\estwth_i)
  +
  \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal I}
  \lambda_{j}
  c^{u}_{j}(\estwth)
  ,
  \label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
where $[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}$ and $[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}$ denote the Lagrange multipliers for the weight constraints \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const1} and input constraints \eqref{eq:opt:prob:const2}, respectively.
Then, the primal-dual problem can be formulated as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:opt:prob:saddle}
  \min_{\estwth}
  \max_{
    [\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
    ,
    [\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
  } 
  L(\mv{e},\estwth,[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}},[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal I})
  .
\end{equation}

The adaptation law\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{s}\DIFdelend  for the adaptive variables, $\estwth$, $[\sigma_i]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}$, and $[\lambda_j]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}$, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{are }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{is }\DIFaddend derived to solve \eqref{eq:opt:prob:saddle} by using a primal-dual gradient update law, \ie 
$
  \ddtt\estwth 
  = 
  -\alpha \pptfrac{L}{\estwth}
$, $
  \ddtt\sigma_i 
  = 
  \myproj_{\sigma_i\ge0}
  \left(
      \beta_i^\theta 
      \pptfrac{L}{\sigma_i}
  \right)
$, and $
  \ddtt\lambda_j 
  = 
  \myproj_{\lambda_j\ge0}
  \left(
    \beta_j^u 
    \pptfrac{L}{\lambda_j}
  \right)
$, as follows:
\begin{subequations} \label{eq:adap}
  \begin{align}
    \ddtt{\estwth}
    &
    =
    -\alpha 
    \left(
      \pptfrac{J}{\estwth}
      +
      \textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
      \sigma_i 
      \estwth_i
      +
      \textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
      \lambda_j 
      \pptfrac{c^{u}_{j}}{\estwth}
    \right),
    \label{eq:adap:th}
    \\
    \ddtt\sigma_i
    & 
    = 
    \myproj_{\sigma_i\ge0}
      \left(
      \beta_i^{\theta}
      c^{\theta}_{i} 
    \right)
    ,
    \qquad \forall i\in\mathcal{K},
    \label{eq:adap:sig}
    \\
    \ddtt\lambda_j
    & 
    = 
    \myproj_{\lambda_j\ge0}
      \left(
      \beta_j^{u}
      c^{u}_{j} 
    \right)
    ,
    \qquad \forall j\in\mathcal{I},
    \label{eq:adap:lbd}
  \end{align}%
\end{subequations}
where $\alpha\in\R_{>0}$ denotes the adaptation gain (learning rate) and $\beta_i^{\theta},\beta_j^{u}\in\R_{>0},\ \forall i\in\mathcal{K},j\in\mathcal{I}$, denote the update rates of the Lagrange multipliers. 
The Lagrange multipliers associated with inequality constraints are kept non-negative, \ie $\sigma_i,\lambda_j\ge 0$, due to \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{the projection operator }\DIFdelend $\myproj_{(\cdot)\ge0}(\cdot)$ in \eqref{eq:adap:sig} and \eqref{eq:adap:lbd}\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, which is presented in \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite[Appendix E, eq. (E.4)]{Krstic:1995aa}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFdelend .

When a constraint becomes violated, \ie positive constraint violation \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$c^{\theta}_{i},c^{u}_{j}\ge0$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$c^{\theta}_{i},c^{u}_{j}>0$}\DIFaddend , the corresponding Lagrange multiplier increases according to \eqref{eq:adap:sig} and \eqref{eq:adap:lbd}, to address the violation by updating weight $\estwth$ through \eqref{eq:adap:th}.
Ultimately, the Lagrange multiplier exhibits two distinct behaviors depending on the constraint status: it either decreases to zero (\eg via a projection operator) when the constraint becomes inactive, $c^{\theta}_i,c^{u}_j < 0$, or converges to a positive value when the constraint remains binding, $c^{\theta}_i,c^{u}_j = 0$, to enforce the limit.

\begin{remark}[Comparison with Existing Methods for Weight Boundedness] \label{rem:weight:bound}
  Unlike projection-based methods that directly restrict the adaptive weights to a prescribed set, or $\sigma$-modification \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, or }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and }\DIFaddend $\epsilon$-modification that continuously bias them toward the origin \cite{Ryu:2025ab}, CONAC handles weight boundedness through inequality constraints within the primal-dual adaptation framework like the input constraints.
  The corresponding constraint term is only activated when the prescribed bound is violated, thereby softly driving the weights toward the admissible set without direct clipping or persistent origin-directed bias.
\end{remark}

Meanwhile, the adaptation law of $\estwth$ defined in \eqref{eq:adap:th} involves the partial derivative of the error with respect to the control input $\pptfrac{\mv{e}}{\mv{u}}$, \ie 
$
    \pptfrac{J}{\estwth}
    =
    \big(

        (\pptfrac{\mv{u}}{\estwth})

\begin{lem}[Boundedness of $\Delta\mv{u}$ with respect to $\estwth_k$] \label{lem:delta:u:bound}
  The saturation of control input $\Delta\mv{u}$ is bounded by $\estwth_k$ as 
  \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\norm{\Delta\mv{u}}\le \overline{p}\sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}$.
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation*}
    \norm{\Delta\mv{u}}
    \le 
    \overline{p}
    \sqrt{l_k+1}
    \norm{\estwth_k}
    .
  \end{equation*}
\DIFaddend \end{lem}

\begin{proof}
  Since $\act_k(\cdot)$ employs the bounded activation function $\vert\tanh(\cdot)\vert < 1$, we have 
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\act_k(\cdot)} \le \sqrt{l_k+1}
    ,
  \end{equation}
  for any output of the preceding layers and $\NNin$.
  Hence, invoking the relation in \eqref{eq:NN:linear}, the control input $\mv{u}=\mm{P}\estNN$ is bounded by $\estwth_k$ as follows:
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:NN:bound}
    \norm{\mv{u}} 
    \le
    \overline{p}
    \norm{\estNN}
    \le
    \overline{p}
    \sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}
    .   
  \end{equation}

  In addition, since there exists $\underline{u}>0$ such that $\inf_{\mv u\notin\Omega_u}\allowbreak(\norm{\mysat(\mv u)})=\underline{u}$ by Assumption~\ref{assum:sat}, we have
  $
    \norm{\Delta\mv u}
    \le
    \norm{\mv u}-\underline{u}
    ,
  $ 
  while $\Delta\mv u=\mv 0$ when $\mv u\in\Omega_u$.
  Thus, we have
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\Delta\mv{u}}
    \le
    \max
    \left\{
      \overline{p}\sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}
      -
      \underline{u}
      ,
      0
    \right\}
    \le
    \overline{p}\sqrt{l_k+1}\norm{\estwth_k}
    ,  
  \end{equation}
  which concludes the proof.    
\end{proof}

\begin{lem} \label{lem:proj:ineq}
  For any $a,b\in\R_{\ge0}$ and $c\in\R_{>0}$, the following inequality holds:
  \begin{equation}
    (a-b)\myproj_{a\ge0}(c) \le (a-b) c.
  \end{equation}
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
  The inequality can be verified by considering the two cases of $a>0$ and $a=0$, as follows:
  \DIFdelbegin %DIFDELCMD < \begin{equation*}
%DIFDELCMD <     (a-b)\myproj_{a\ge0}(c) 
%DIFDELCMD <     =
%DIFDELCMD <     \begin{cases}
%DIFDELCMD <       (a-b) c, & \text{if } a>0,
%DIFDELCMD <       \\
%DIFDELCMD <       0, & \text{if } a=0,
%DIFDELCMD <     \end{cases}
%DIFDELCMD <   \end{equation*}%%%
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation*}
    (a-b)\myproj_{a\ge0}(c) 
    =
    \begin{cases}
      (a-b) c, & \text{if } a>0,
      \\
      0, & \text{if } a=0.
    \end{cases}
  \end{equation*}\DIFaddend 
  This implies that $(a-b)\myproj_{a\ge0}(c) \le (a-b) c$ always holds and concludes the proof.
\end{proof}

The following theorem establishes the UUB of the tracking error $\mv{e}$, the DNN weights $\estwth$, and the Lagrange multipliers $\sigma_i,\ \forall i \in \mathcal{K}$, and $\lambda_j,\ \forall j \in \mathcal{I}$.

\begin{theorem} \label{thm:stability}
  Consider the closed-loop system consisting of \eqref{eq:sys}, the control law \eqref{eq:ctrl:NN:actual}, and the adaptation law\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{s}\DIFdelend  \eqref{eq:adap}.
  Suppose that Assumptions~\ref{assum:B:bound}--\ref{assum:xd} hold and the initial condition belongs to the compact invariant set 
  \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$
  \bigcap_{i\in\mathcal K}
  \mathcal S_i(\overline V_i)
  $ 
  }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$
  \prod_{\ell=0}^k \mathcal{S}_\ell(\overline V_\ell)
  $ 
  }\DIFaddend constructed in \eqref{eq:Sk:def} and \eqref{eq:Si:def}.
  Then, the tracking error $\mv e$, the DNN weights $\estwth$, and the Lagrange multipliers $\sigma_i,\ \forall i \in \mathcal{K},$ and $\lambda_j,\ \forall j \in \mathcal{I},$ are uniformly ultimately bounded.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}

The proof is divided into two steps.
We first analyze the UBB of the tracking error $\mv{e}$, the output-layer adaptive variables, $\estwth_k$, and $\sigma_k$, and the Lagrange multipliers associated with the input constraints $\lambda_j,\ \forall j\in\mathcal{I}$.
This step provides the key boundedness result for the tracking error and the controller output.
In the following step, we analyze the remaining inner-layer adaptive variables, $\estwth_i$ and $\sigma_i,\ \forall i\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{k\}$.

Additionally, we introduce the following remarks regarding the initialization and the parameter tuning of CONAC.

\begin{remark}[Initialization of CONAC] \label{rem:init}
  The invariant sublevel set 
  \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$
    \bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{K}} 
    \mathcal{S}_i(\overline{V}_i)
  $ 
  }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$
    \prod_{\ell=0}^k \mathcal{S}_\ell(\overline V_\ell)
  $ 
  }\DIFaddend used in the stability analysis depends on unknown constants.
  Therefore, the exact admissible initial domain cannot be computed explicitly.
  In practice, we recommend initializing; the system state $\mv{x}(0)$ close to the initial reference state $\mv{x}_d(0)$ to reduce the initial tracking error $\mv{e}(0)$; DNN weights with small random values; and zero for the Lagrange multipliers, $[\sigma_i(0)]_{i\in\mathcal{K}}=\mv{0}$ and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$[\lambda_j(0)]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}=\mv{0}$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$[\lambda_j(0)]_{j\in\mathcal{I}}=\mv{0},$ }\DIFaddend without constraint violations, to help ensure that the system starts within
  \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$
    \bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{K}} 
    \mathcal{S}_i(\overline{V}_i)
    .
  $
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$
    \prod_{\ell=0}^k \mathcal{S}_\ell(\overline V_\ell)
    .
  $
}\DIFaddend \end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Initial Desired Trajectory Design] \label{rem:ref}  
  The initialization in Remark~\ref{rem:init} helps prevent initial unstable behavior, which generally results from poor initial performance of NAC due to the randomly initialized DNN weights.
  With small random weights, the initial control input is expected to be small, thereby avoiding abrupt control actions.
  However, if the system starts near an unstable or highly nonlinear operating region, small random weights may result in unstable behavior or slow adaptation.
  Hence, it is preferable to design the desired trajectory such that the system starts in a sufficiently stable operating region \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(}\eg \DIFadd{near the stable equilibrium point)}\DIFaddend , and use a conservative desired trajectory during an initial warm-up phase, as introduced in \cite{Ryu:2025aa}.
  % This strategy is demonstrated across valuations in Section~\ref{sec:val}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Tuning of CONAC Parameters] \label{rem:tuning}
  The adaptation gain $\alpha$ and the multiplier update rates $\beta_i^\theta$ and $\beta_j^u$ affect the transient behavior of CONAC.
  The gain $\alpha$ determines the overall update speed of the DNN weights.
  A large $\alpha$ may improve the initial learning response but can also induce overshooting or oscillatory behavior, resulting in failure to learn the ideal weights.
  Conversely, an excessively small $\alpha$ may lead to slow adaptation and poor transient tracking performance.
  The update rates $\beta_i^\theta$ and $\beta_j^u$ determine how rapidly the Lagrange multipliers respond to weight and input constraint violations, respectively.
  Larger values accelerate the enforcement of the corresponding constraints by increasing the multiplier response when a violation occurs.
  However, excessively large update rates may introduce abrupt changes in the adaptation law, leading to chattering or high-frequency oscillations in the control input on the constraint boundary.

  Therefore, $\alpha$, $\beta_i^\theta$, and $\beta_j^u$ should be selected to balance learning speed, prompt constraint enforcement, and smooth closed-loop behavior.
  The effect of the input constraint multiplier update rate $\beta_j^u$ is evaluated in the parametric study in Section~\ref{sec:val:results}.
\end{remark}

To validate the proposed CONAC, a real-time experiment was conducted on a two-link robotic manipulator, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:exp:real:set}.

The two-link robotic manipulator dynamics are described by the following Euler-Lagrange equation:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:robot:dynamics}

The inertia matrix $\mv{M}(\mv{q})$ whose inverse is the control effectiveness matrix, is a symmetric positive-definite matrix according to the physical properties of Euler-Lagrange equation, \cite[Lemma 3.1]{Ji:2025aa}.

\subsubsection*{Controller 3 \normalfont{(C$_3$)}—\textit{NAC with auxiliary system}}

As a baseline for comparison in handling input constraints \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{in NAC}\DIFaddend , the auxiliary system used in \cite{Rezaei:2025aa,Liu:2025aa,Yu:2022aa} was adopted.
% Gao:2017aa,Esfandiari:2014aa,Esfandiari:2015aa,Liu:2024aa,Liu:2025aa,Yu:2022aa,Rezaei:2025aa
It should be noted that the auxiliary system is limited to handling only the element-wise input bound constraints.
The auxiliary system is defined by 
\begin{equation}
  \ddtt \mv{\zeta} 
  =
  \mm{A}_{\zeta}
  \mv{\zeta}
  +
  \mm{B}_{\zeta}
  \Delta_{\zeta} \mv{\tau}
  ,
  \quad
  \zeta(0) 
  = 
  \mv{0}_{2\times 1}
  ,
\end{equation}
where $\mv{\zeta}\in\R^2$ denotes the auxiliary state which is initialized to zero, $\mm{A}_{\zeta}\in\R^{2\times 2}$ is a stable matrix, $\mm{B}_{\zeta}\in\R^{2\times 2}$ is a gain matrix, and $\Delta_{\zeta} \tau_i := \tau_i - \max\{\min\{\tau_i, \overline{\tau}_i\}, -\overline{\tau}_i\},\ \forall i\in\{1,2\}$.
% \operatorname{clip}(u_i, \underline{u}_i, \overline{u}_i),\ \forall i\in\{1,2\}$.
It is notable that \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\Delta_{\zeta}\mv{u}=\Delta_{\zeta}\mv{\tau}$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\Delta_{\zeta}\mv{\tau}$ }\DIFaddend is defined as the element-wise input overflow, which is different from the actual input saturation $\Delta\mv{u}$ in \eqref{eq:Vc:dot:1}.
% The limits of the control input are set as $\underline{u}_1=-\overline{u}_1=10.428$ and $\underline{u}_2=-\overline{u}_2=3.5$, to approximate the input norm constraint $\overline{\tau} = 11$.
The auxiliary state was used in the adaptation law \eqref{eq:adap:th:approx}, by substituting the tracking error $\mv{e}$ with the auxiliary error $\mv{e}_\zeta := \mv{e} + \mv{\zeta}$.
The parameters of the auxiliary system were chosen to balance the responsiveness to input overflow with the convergence speed of the auxiliary dynamics, as listed in Table~\ref{table:controller}.
All other properties and parameters were kept the same as in (C$_1$) and (C$_2$).

Consequently, the DNN weights exhibited persistent growth rather than convergence as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ctrl:result:general:weight}, which may eventually lead to parameter drift or unbounded weight growth and is therefore undesirable.

In this section, the constraint-handling performance of the controllers is analyzed.
For a detailed analysis, we focus on the time interval $[\zoomStartTime, \qty{\zoomEndTime}{\second}]$ of Episode $2$ which is highlighted by the green dashed lines in Fig.~\ref{fig:ctrl:result:general}, where the difference in the considered input constraints among the controllers is most pronounced.
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ctrl:result:detail:q:top:view}, the tracking errors of all controllers were observed to be increased due to the input saturation effect, but (C$_1$) and (C$_2$) exhibited smaller tracking errors compared to (C$_3$) and (C$_4$).
In Fig.~\ref{fig:ctrl:result:detail:u:top:view}, the commanded control inputs of (C$_1$) and (C$_2$) were observed to properly handle the input constraints, using the full authority of the actuator.
In contrast, the commanded control input of (C$_3$) was saturated at the box constraints, which is attributed to the design of the auxiliary system that only constrains the element-wise input overflow, rather than the actual input saturation, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:exp:sat:cmp}.
Finally, the commanded control input of (C$_4$) was observed to exceed the input limits, which is expected since (C$_4$) does not consider input constraints in its adaptation process.

The parametric study of the Lagrange multiplier update rates is conducted by comparing (C$_1$) and (C$_2$), focusing on the multiplier associated with the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{input norm }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{power source limit }\DIFaddend constraint $\lambda_{\overline{\tau}}$.
In Fig.~\ref{fig:ctrl:result:detail:multipliers}, $\lambda_{\overline{\tau}}$ of (C$_1$) and (C$_2$) was observed to be non-zero, indicating that the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{input norm }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{power source limit }\DIFaddend constraint was active and enforced during this time interval.
Specifically, the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_{\overline{\tau}}$ of (C$_1$), which had a larger update rate, repeatedly grew and decayed, while that of (C$_2$) remained relatively constant.
Consequently, a larger update rate $\beta^u_{\overline{\tau}}$ provides a faster response to the input constraint but induces greater oscillations in the Lagrange multiplier, whereas a smaller update rate yields smoother but less responsive constraint enforcement.
This result confirms the trade-off discussed in Remark~\ref{rem:tuning}.

Additionally, the nearly constant value of $\lambda_{\overline{\tau}}$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:ctrl:result:detail:multipliers} and the small oscillations of $c_{\overline{\tau}}^u$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:ctrl:result:detail:u:top:view} of (C$_2$) suggest that the adaptation dynamics remain near an equilibrium characterized by a nonzero Lagrange multiplier.
This behavior is qualitatively consistent with the KKT-like optimality conditions discussed in Section~\ref{sec:main:result:sub:adap:laws}, according to which, at an equilibrium, the positive Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_{\overline{\tau}}$ is associated with active constraint satisfying $c_{\overline{\tau}}^u=0$ through the complementary slackness relation $\lambda_{\overline{\tau}}c_{\overline{\tau}}^u=0$.
Although the adaptation dynamics do not converge exactly to a stationary KKT equilibrium because of the practical limitations in the real-time implementation, the observed behavior can be interpreted as a near-equilibrium behavior.

\DIFaddbegin \subsection{\DIFadd{Discussion on the Advantages and Limitations of CONAC}}

\DIFadd{In the validations, we demonstrated that CONAC can handle multiple convex input constraints simultaneously, while the other NAC methods failed to do so.
However, it is important to note that the proposed CONAC is only able to handle the constraints in soft manner, meaning that the constraints may be violated during the adaptation process.
One may impose a conservative constraint considering the potential violation of the constraints, but this may lead to a suboptimal solution.
In safety perspective, some constraints must be satisfied without violation in operation time.
Consequently, extension of the proposed CONAC to incorporate hard constraints remains an important direction for future work.
}

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

This paper proposed CONAC for unknown MIMO systems, addressing multiple convex input constraints within a unified constrained optimization framework. 
The adaptation \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{laws were }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{law was }\DIFaddend derived from the constrained optimization problem, which was formulated to minimize the tracking error while satisfying the multiple convex input constraints.
The stability of CONAC was analyzed using Lyapunov theory, demonstrating the boundedness of the tracking errors and adaptive variables, including the DNN weights and Lagrange multipliers.
By deriving adaptation law\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{s}\DIFdelend  from the constrained optimization problem, CONAC ensured weight convergence in accordance with the KKT optimality conditions, thereby ensuring both stability and optimality.

Real-time implementation demonstrated the capability of CONAC in handling multiple convex input constraints simultaneously, while achieving superior tracking performance compared to other NAC methods.
Moreover, the parametric study revealed the effect of the update rates of the Lagrange multipliers on the enforcement of input constraints.
In addition, the experiments validated the feasibility of CONAC in real-time implementation.

Future work may extend this approach to \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{include constraintsons system states, and minimize the infinite horizon error cost, thereby further enhancing the capability and optimality of NAC systems within constrained optimization frameworks}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{handle hard constraints, such as state constraints, in addition to input constraints. 
Moreover, by incorporating reinforcement learning into the optimization problem formulation and deriving adaptation law that explicitly accounts for disturbances, the proposed CONAC framework could be extended to infinite-horizon optimal control problems while further enhancing its robustness}\DIFaddend .
